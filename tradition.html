The Tradition

A reader's path through the yogic and spiritual heritage of Mithila.

For nearly three thousand years, the land between the Gandak and the Kosi has been a quiet, persistent laboratory of contemplative life. Sages have argued under its tamarinds; queens have asked unanswerable questions in its courts; commentators have spent lifetimes on a single verse; village women have painted yogic symbols on wedding walls. What follows is an attempt to gather, in one place, what this region has carried — without invention, without exaggeration, and without claiming what is not ours.

01 · Upanishadic foundations

Yājñavalkya's teaching, at greater length.

The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad — among the oldest and largest of the Upaniṣads, conventionally dated by scholars to the pre-Buddhist period — preserves a series of dialogues set in the court of King Janaka of Videha. The sage Yājñavalkya is its central figure. The teachings he gives there are not yoga as a system of postures; they are something earlier and more fundamental: a sustained inquiry into the witness behind experience.

Neti, neti

Asked again and again to describe the Self, Yājñavalkya refuses every positive answer. The Self, he says, is "not this, not this" — नेति नेति — beyond every quality that thought can put around it. This single phrase has become one of the most consequential teachings in the whole of Indian philosophy. Whole later schools — Advaita Vedānta above all — would unfold from it.

स एष नेति नेत्यात्मा।
अगृह्यो न हि गृह्यते।
अशीर्यो न हि शीर्यते।
असङ्गो न हि सज्यते।

sa eṣa neti netyātmā / agṛhyo na hi gṛhyate / aśīryo na hi śīryate / asaṅgo na hi sajyate

"This Self is not this, not this. Ungraspable, for it is not grasped. Indestructible, for it cannot be destroyed. Unattached, for it does not attach."

— Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.5.15

The four states of consciousness

In the same Upaniṣad, Yājñavalkya describes the four states through which the Self moves: waking (jāgrat), dream (svapna), deep sleep (suṣupti), and the fourth — turīya, the silent witness beyond all three. This fourfold map would later be formalised in the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad and become the structural backbone of much of classical yoga and Vedānta.

The kosṭha and the breath

Yājñavalkya speaks too of the breath as the unifying thread (sūtra) through the body — a teaching the later yoga tradition would extend into the elaborate sciences of prāṇa, nāḍī, and cakra. In Mithila, the breath was first thought of not as a technique but as a question.

What is striking in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka is that Janaka — the king — is the one who asks. The questioner is a householder, a ruler, a man embedded in the world. The yoga of Mithila is, from its earliest moment, a yoga for the householder. The forest is allowed; it is not required.

02 · The Householder Path

Maitreyī and the question of immortality.

Among the most quoted dialogues in the whole of Sanskrit literature is the one in which Yājñavalkya prepares to leave his household and walk into the forest. He calls his two wives and offers to divide his wealth between them. Kātyāyanī accepts. Maitreyī does not.

येनाहं नामृता स्याम् किमहं तेन कुर्याम्।

yenāhaṃ nāmṛtā syām kim ahaṃ tena kuryām

"What should I do with that by which I cannot become immortal?"

— Maitreyī, Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 2.4.3

The dialogue that follows is one of the high points of Indian philosophy. Yājñavalkya teaches Maitreyī that nothing in the world is loved for its own sake — a husband is not loved for the husband's sake, a son not for the son's, wealth not for its own — all are loved for the sake of the Self. The Self alone is what is to be seen, heard, contemplated, meditated upon: आत्मा वा अरे द्रष्टव्यः — "the Self, my dear, is to be beheld."

That Mithila preserved this teaching in the voice of a woman is not incidental. The tradition has, from this earliest stratum, taken the philosophical voice of its women seriously. Maitreyī's question — what good is wealth if it cannot make me deathless? — is the question yoga, in every form, has been trying to answer ever since.

03 · The Aṣṭāvakra Gītā

Janaka's other teacher.

The Aṣṭāvakra Gītā — also called the Aṣṭāvakra Saṃhitā — is among the most uncompromising statements of non-dual realisation in any tradition. It takes the form of a dialogue between King Janaka and the sage Aṣṭāvakra, whose body, the story tells us, was bent in eight places from birth. The text's actual date of composition is debated by scholars (estimates range across many centuries), but its setting is unmistakably Mithila, and its principal speaker is Janaka — the same philosopher-king of the Upaniṣads.

Where the Bhagavad Gītā balances action with knowledge, the Aṣṭāvakra Gītā keeps its gaze fixed on knowledge alone. Liberation, it says, is not a future event; it is already the case, missed only through the habit of identifying with what one is not.

मुक्ताभिमानी मुक्तो हि बद्धो बद्धाभिमान्यपि।
किंवदन्तीह सत्येयं या मतिः सा गतिर्भवेत्॥

muktābhimānī mukto hi baddho baddhābhimāny api / kiṃvadantīha satyeyaṃ yā matiḥ sā gatir bhavet

"He who thinks himself free is free; he who thinks himself bound, bound. As one thinks, so one becomes — there is truth in this old saying."

— Aṣṭāvakra Gītā 1.11

That a tradition centred on the householder Janaka could give rise to a text this radical is part of Mithila's peculiar gift. The world is not renounced here; it is simply seen through.

04 · The Yoga Sūtras, from Mithila

Vācaspati Miśra.

There is a fact most yoga students never learn: one of the two indispensable classical commentaries on Patañjali's Yoga Sūtras was written by a man of Mithila.

Vācaspati Miśra, who flourished in the ninth or tenth century, was a Maithil philosopher of staggering range. He wrote major commentaries on all six classical darśanas — Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā, and Vedānta — earning him the title ṣaḍ-darśana-vallabha, "the beloved of the six systems." His Yoga Sūtra commentary, the Tattva-Vaiśāradī, is a sub-commentary on the Vyāsa Bhāṣya and remains, eleven centuries on, one of the two texts every serious student of the Sūtras reads.

It is one of the better-kept secrets of yoga's intellectual history that when later teachers and translators explain Patañjali, they are very often, knowingly or not, drawing on the work of a scholar from this region.

When you read a careful modern translation of the Yoga Sūtras, much of what makes the text intelligible — its careful distinctions, its precise vocabulary of citta, vṛtti, samādhi — comes through the lens Vācaspati Miśra polished here in Mithila.

Vācaspati's other works include the Bhāmatī (his commentary on Śaṅkara's Brahma Sūtra Bhāṣya, which founded the Bhāmatī sub-school of Advaita — see Section 06), and the Nyāya-vārttika-tātparya-ṭīkā on Nyāya logic. His scholarly habit — quiet, thorough, generous to opponents — became something of a Maithil temperament in itself.

05 · The Mithila School of Nyāya

Where logic became a contemplative discipline.

From roughly the twelfth to the sixteenth century, Mithila was the principal centre of Indian logic. The system — Nyāya — had existed since the early centuries of the Common Era. What happened in Mithila was that it was renewed, sharpened, and turned, in the right hands, into something close to a spiritual practice.

Udayana

The eleventh-century Maithil logician Udayana wrote, among other works, the Nyāya-kusumāñjali — "A Handful of Flowers of Reason" — a sustained philosophical defence of the existence of God against Buddhist critics. The text is not merely apologetic; it is one of the most careful and beautiful pieces of natural theology in any tradition.

Gaṅgeśa Upādhyāya and the Navya-Nyāya

In the fourteenth century, the Maithil philosopher Gaṅgeśa Upādhyāya wrote the Tattvacintāmaṇi — "The Wish-Granting Jewel of Reality" — which founded what came to be known as Navya-Nyāya, the "new logic." Gaṅgeśa was, by general scholarly agreement, one of the most important Indian philosophers of the second millennium.

What Gaṅgeśa achieved is hard to describe to readers outside the tradition. He developed a technical language for reasoning so precise that some Western logicians, encountering it in the twentieth century, recognised in it analogues of the formal logic developed in Europe only after Frege. The Navya-Nyāya remained the standard idiom of Sanskrit philosophy for the next five centuries; even Vedāntins and grammarians, who disagreed with everything else Maithil logicians said, wrote in their vocabulary.

Logic as contemplative practice

It is easy to imagine logic as the opposite of yoga — cold where yoga is warm, public where yoga is inward. But the Maithil tradition treated tarka (reasoning) and dhyāna (meditation) as siblings. A pandit's entire day was a discipline of attention: a sustained refusal to be carried away by sloppy thought. The mind that has been trained on Navya-Nyāya is a mind that is much harder to fool.

06 · Mandana Miśra & the Bhāmatī

The debate at Mahiṣī.

In the traditional biographies of Śaṅkarācārya — composed several centuries after his life — there is a famous episode in which the young Śaṅkara, walking through the country teaching Advaita, comes to the village of Mahiṣī in Mithila to debate the Mīmāṃsā scholar Mandana Miśra. The debate lasts many days. Mandana's wife, Ubhaya Bhāratī, serves as the judge — a Maithil woman whose philosophical learning was such that both men accepted her as arbiter.

How much of this is history, how much is later tradition, is a question scholars have not settled. What is certain is that the Bhāmatī school of Advaita Vedānta — one of the two great sub-schools that interpret Śaṅkara's work — takes its name from Vācaspati Miśra's commentary written here in Mithila (see Section 04), and that the figures of Mandana Miśra and Ubhaya Bhāratī have remained, for over a millennium, part of how India remembers the philosophical seriousness of this region.

That the judge of the most famous philosophical debate in Indian memory was a Maithil woman is a fact the tradition has chosen, very deliberately, to preserve.

Mahiṣī itself, still a small village in the Saharsa district of Bihar, retains a temple to the goddess Ugratārā — one of the Mahāvidyās — and the site continues to be visited by scholars, pilgrims, and the merely curious.

07 · Tantric Mithila

The fierce goddess and the household altar.

Alongside its philosophical seriousness, Mithila has been, for at least a thousand years, a centre of Śākta and Tāntric practice — the worship of the goddess in her many forms. The two streams are not in opposition. Many of the same Maithil pandits who wrote Nyāya treatises in the day worshipped Kālī and Tārā at night.

The Mahāvidyās

The tradition of the Ten Mahāvidyās — ten fierce manifestations of the great goddess — has deep roots in this region. The temple of Ugratārā at Mahiṣī, mentioned above, is one of the most venerable Tārā shrines in eastern India. Local tradition counts it among the siddha-pīṭhas — places where realisation is said to come more readily because the work of countless practitioners has charged the ground.

Household tantra

What is distinctive in Mithila is that this Tantric practice has not stayed in temples and renunciate orders alone. It has woven itself into the everyday rituals of Maithil households — into the wedding chamber (kohbar), the consecration of the family altar, the songs sung at the daughter's farewell. The geometry of yantra, the iconography of the goddess, the syllables of her mantras are all there, half-hidden in the painted walls and embroidered cloth of village life.

Mithila's tantra is, in this sense, of a piece with its yoga: an inward discipline that has refused, again and again, to abandon the world it sits in.

08 · Vidyāpati

Devotion in the mother tongue.

Around the turn of the fifteenth century, a poet of the Maithil court named Vidyāpati Ṭhākura began composing devotional songs in what was then a vernacular: the Maithili language, the speech of his own villages. The decision was, in its way, a revolution. Up to that point, the high tradition of devotion had been almost entirely in Sanskrit. Vidyāpati's songs to Śiva and to the figures of the Kṛṣṇa-Rādhā tradition placed the experience of God in the mouth — and the voice, and the music — of ordinary Maithil speech.

His influence was enormous. The Bengali Vaiṣṇavas of the next century — Caitanya Mahāprabhu and his followers — counted Vidyāpati among the foundational singers of the tradition. His padāvalī have been sung continuously in Maithil and Bengali households for more than five hundred years.

Bhakti in this region

For all his courtly position, Vidyāpati's lyrics carry the directness of folk song. He could write a metaphysical treatise (he did — the Puruṣa-parīkṣā) and then, in the same week, a song about Rādhā longing through a monsoon afternoon. The Maithil temperament has not, in the centuries since, found these two things contradictory.

To hear Vidyāpati sung in a Maithil house at dawn is to hear, in the same single voice, an inheritance that runs unbroken to the Bṛhadāraṇyaka.

09 · The living tradition

What survives, daily.

A tradition is not a text; it is what people do on a Tuesday morning. In Mithila, the older yogic and devotional heritage has survived, more than anywhere else, through practices most outsiders do not recognise as yoga at all.

Chhath

The four-day festival of Chhath, held twice a year on the banks of every Maithil pond and river, is — at its core — a sun-meditation, performed in cold water at dawn and dusk, in fast and silence. It is the largest living daily sūrya-upāsana in the world. Its discipline (no metal, no salt, no shelter, no shoes, sleep on the floor) is closer to the austerity of the Upaniṣadic ṛṣis than anything one is likely to see in a city ashram.

Vivāha Pañcamī

The annual commemoration of the marriage of Sītā and Rāma — held on the fifth day of the bright half of Mārgaśīrṣa — is observed across Mithila with weeks of preparation. The festival joins this region with Janakpur, in present-day Nepal, where Sītā is traditionally said to have been born. The two places have been twinned for as long as the tradition remembers.

Kohbar Ghar and Mithila painting

The bridal chamber in a traditional Maithil house is painted — by the women — with a dense iconography: lotuses, fish, the sun and moon, the bamboo grove, the parrot. Almost every motif has a yogic or tantric meaning underneath. The art form that the world now knows as Madhubani painting began here, in this domestic ritual practice, before it became a globally recognised craft.

The household pandit

Until recently, almost every village in Mithila had at least one paṇḍita: a Sanskrit scholar who taught the children of the village, recited the texts at funerals and weddings, kept the panchang, and could be relied on for an argument about Nyāya at any hour. This figure is now rarer than he was. Part of what Mithila Yoga exists for is to make sure that what he carried does not vanish.

10 · A reading list

For those who wish to follow this further.

If something in the above has caught your attention, the following are starting points — accessible translations and serious scholarship in roughly equal measure. We can recommend more on request.

Primary texts in translation

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad. Patrick Olivelle's translation in The Early Upaniṣads (Oxford, 1998) is the modern standard; Swami Madhavananda's older Advaita Ashrama edition includes Śaṅkara's commentary.
  • The Aṣṭāvakra Gītā. Several good translations exist; Swami Nityaswarupananda's bilingual edition is widely used.
  • The Yoga Sūtras of Patañjali, with Vyāsa's Bhāṣya and Vācaspati Miśra's Tattva-Vaiśāradī. The translation by Rāma Prasāda (1910) is in the public domain; modern editions are available.
  • Vidyāpati's Padāvalī. Many anthologies exist in Hindi, Bengali, and English. Deben Bhattacharya's Love Songs of Vidyāpati remains a fine entry point.

Scholarship

  • Bimal Krishna Matilal. The Character of Logic in India — the indispensable introduction to Nyāya, including the Mithila school.
  • Jonardon Ganeri. Philosophy in Classical India, and The Lost Age of Reason, for the wider intellectual context of Mithila's contributions.
  • Karl H. Potter (ed.). Encyclopedia of Indian Philosophies, volumes on Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika and on Advaita Vedānta — exhaustive bibliographic and doctrinal coverage of Vācaspati Miśra, Udayana, Gaṅgeśa, and others.
  • Radha Krishna Choudhary. A Survey of Maithili Literature — for Vidyāpati and the broader Maithili literary tradition.
  • David Kinsley. Tantric Visions of the Divine Feminine — for the Mahāvidyās and the goddess traditions of eastern India.

This list is not exhaustive and will grow as the Archives grow. Scholars are welcome to suggest additions.
